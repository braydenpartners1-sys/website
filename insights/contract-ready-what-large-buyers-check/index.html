Black-owned businesses

Contract-ready: what large buyers check before they work with a smaller supplier

Large organisations increasingly want more diverse supply chains. Winning their business often depends less on your pitch than on whether your business passes their checks.

Many large companies and public bodies now actively look for smaller and more diverse suppliers. For Black-owned businesses, that is a real opportunity. Organisations such as MSDUK, which has connected ethnic minority-owned suppliers with corporate buyers since 2006, exist precisely to open those doors.

But an open door is not a contract. MSDUK itself is clear that its certification "does not confer preferential treatment, guarantee contracts, or influence supplier selection decisions". Every supplier still goes through the same checks. And that is where many capable small businesses fall down.

Where it usually goes wrong

It is rarely the pitch. The buyer likes the proposal, the price works, the meeting goes well. Then a supplier questionnaire arrives asking for documents and evidence the business has never needed before. Deadlines slip, gaps appear, and the opportunity quietly goes to someone else.

The good news is that most of what buyers ask for is predictable. If you prepare it before the opportunity arrives, the checks become a formality.

What buyers typically check

Requirements vary by buyer and by contract, but the same areas come up again and again.

Who you are. Your company registration, ownership and the people who run the business. For UK public sector work, suppliers now register on the government's central digital platform and submit their "core supplier information" once, rather than filling in the same details for every tender.

Financial standing. Buyers want to know you can deliver and will still be trading at the end of the contract. Public bodies cannot insist on audited accounts as a condition of taking part, but they will look at other evidence of your financial capacity. Up-to-date accounts or clear management accounts make this easy.

Insurance. Employers' Liability insurance is a legal requirement once you employ people. Buyers often also ask about public liability and professional indemnity cover. Under the current public procurement rules, a public body cannot require contract-level insurance before awarding the contract, but it can require you to have it in place when the contract starts. Know what you would need and how quickly you could get it.

Policies. Expect questions about equality and diversity, health and safety, and how you handle personal data. If you have five or more employees, your health and safety policy must be written down. Most organisations that use personal information must also pay the ICO's data protection fee unless they are exempt.

Information security. Cyber Essentials is the government-backed baseline for protecting against common cyber attacks. Since October 2014 it has been mandatory for central government contracts that involve handling personal information or providing certain ICT products and services, and the National Cyber Security Centre notes that a growing number of organisations ask suppliers to be certified.

Your supply chain. Only organisations with a turnover of £36 million or more must publish a modern slavery statement. But large buyers do have to account for risks in their supply chains, so be ready to explain how you choose and manage your own suppliers.

Track record. Short, specific examples of similar work, and people willing to act as references.

The public sector: worth a closer look

The Procurement Act 2023 came into force on 24 February 2025, and it contains measures that matter to smaller suppliers. Public bodies must have regard to the barriers small and medium-sized businesses face and consider whether they can be removed or reduced. Public contracts also carry an implied term that valid invoices are paid within 30 days.

High-value opportunities, usually above £139,688 including VAT, are advertised on the Find a Tender service. Contracts worth over £12,000 with central government and its agencies appear on Contracts Finder. Both are free to search.

How to become contract-ready

  1. Build a supplier pack. Gather your registration details, latest accounts, insurance certificates, core policies, data protection registration and two or three short case studies in one place.
  2. Close the gaps in order. Start with the items almost every buyer asks for: insurance, financial evidence and core policies. Then add certifications such as Cyber Essentials if your target clients expect them.
  3. Get visible. Register on the platforms your target buyers use. If your business is at least 51% owned by people from an ethnic minority background, is registered at Companies House and the owners are active in running it, MSDUK certification may help you meet corporate buyers directly.
  4. Keep it current. Insurance renews, policies date and certifications expire. A supplier pack that was accurate last year can let you down this year.

Open the door, then walk through it

Supplier diversity programmes create introductions. Being contract-ready turns those introductions into work. The businesses that win are usually not the biggest in the room, but the ones that can say yes to the buyer's checklist without a scramble.
